# Ukraine's IT Services Exports, 2022–2025, and the Outlook for 2026

*Prepared for a nearshore delivery partnership decision. Data as of mid-November 2026.*

## Executive Summary

Ukraine's IT services exports are large and resilient, but they are no longer a growth story. Computer services exports peaked at about $7.3 billion in 2022, fell to $6.7 billion in 2023 and about $6.4 billion in 2024, and then recovered modestly to $6.66 billion in 2025 (+3.3%) [1][2][5]. Even so, the sector remains Ukraine's second-largest export category after agri-food, at 12.3% of all goods and services exports and 41.6% of services exports in 2025 [1].

The first nine months of 2026 point to a flat-to-slightly-up year. H1 2026 exports were $3.34 billion (+4.1% y/y) [7], while Q3 was $1.67 billion (+1.3%), with September actually 0.9% below September 2025 [8].

For a buyer, the picture is: a deep, senior talent pool of roughly 300,000 specialists, a tax and legal regime (Diia.City) built for exporters, and a customer base dominated by the US and Europe. Against that sit wartime operational risk, a shrinking sole-proprietor base, mobilization pressure, and an ageing workforce. **Our assessment: a partnership is viable and commercially sensible, provided it is structured with a Ukraine-plus-EU delivery footprint, continuity clauses, and a focus on senior-heavy, specialized work rather than commodity capacity.**

## 1. How Big Are the Exports?

| Year | IT/computer services exports (USD) | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | ~$7.3 bn (record) | — |
| 2023 | ~$6.7 bn | -8.4% [5] |
| 2024 | ~$6.4–6.45 bn | -4.3% [2][3] |
| 2025 | $6.66 bn | +3.3% (+$210 m) [1] |

All figures derive from National Bank of Ukraine (NBU) balance-of-payments data as republished by industry bodies. By our own calculation, 2025 exports sit roughly 9% below the 2022 peak in nominal terms (and further below in real terms).

Sector turnover is larger than exports because of the domestic market: IT turnover was about $7.48 billion in 2024 (-5.3%), and the sector's share of GDP slipped from 4.43% in 2023 to 3.92% in 2024 [3]. BDO's 2026 digital-recovery review estimates the sector's total economic impact at about $12.2 billion, including roughly $6.4 billion of gross value added (about 3.9% of GDP) [6].

## 2. How Exports Changed During the War

**2022: peak, then shock.** Exports hit a historical maximum in 2022 despite the full-scale invasion in February, helped by clients accelerating contracts with Ukrainian teams and rapid relocation of staff and operations [5].

**2023–2024: gradual erosion, not collapse.** Exports declined 8.4% in 2023 and a further 4.3% in 2024 [2][5]. Industry analysts describe the decline as gradual and tied to wartime conditions and global demand for IT services [2]. Monthly values were volatile around contract timing: 2024 ranged from $507 million (August) to $617 million (December) [2]. In 2024 IT's share of GDP fell and the domestic market weakened in dollar terms because of hryvnia devaluation [3].

**2025: stabilization.** Quarterly exports rose through the year: Q1 $1.57 billion (-1.3% y/y, the weakest), Q2 $1.64 billion, Q3 $1.65 billion (+5.2%), and Q4 $1.79 billion (+7.6%), with H2 up 7.4% on H1 [1]. December 2025 was the strongest month at $685 million [1]. January 2025 was the weakest at $489 million, versus $528 million in January 2023 [1].

**Market mix.** The US is the single largest buyer. In 2025 it took $2.39 billion, or 35.9% of exports; the UK took $557 million (8.4%) and Israel $264 million (4.0%). Malta ($578 million, 8.7%) and Cyprus ($480 million, 7.2%) rank high largely because many Ukrainian IT firms and their international clients contract through entities there. The top ten markets together accounted for $5.33 billion, or 80.1% [1]. In 2024 the US share was about 37.5% ($2.4 billion), with the UK at $565 million (+5.4% y/y) and Malta at $502 million (-12.9%) [2]. BDO estimates the US at about 36% and Europe at about 51% of IT exports [6].

## 3. Workforce and Industry Structure

- **Headcount:** about 303,000 IT specialists in 2025, of which roughly 245,000 live and work in Ukraine (+2.9%) and about 58,000 work abroad, down from 62,000–64,000 [3]. BDO cites about 305,000 [6]. The sector supports roughly 2.5–2.7 additional jobs elsewhere in the economy [3][6].
- **Seniority:** over 88% are Middle, Senior or Lead level; 43% have 6–15 years of experience and 12% more than 15 [3]. Average age rose from 30.9 (2023) to 32.8 (2025) [3].
- **Companies:** about 2,062 active verified IT companies; 46% are outsourcing-oriented, 31% product, with the rest hybrid [3]. Service companies employ about 48% of specialists [3]. Diia.City reported 3,893 residents by 2026 [6].
- **Pay:** median technical-specialist income about $2,700 per month (+4.2% y/y), with 82.9% reporting higher living expenses [3]. That rate is well below US and Western European levels but is no longer "cheap" for the senior-heavy profile.

## 4. What Drives the Exports

1. **Global demand for senior engineering talent.** Ukraine's experienced, English-capable workforce and long-standing outsourcing relationships with US and European clients sustain demand [2][3].
2. **Wartime customer behavior.** Clients kept Ukrainian teams as their risk-adjusted relocation and diversification efforts progressed; companies opened new locations in 2025 (27% of firms, 23% of them abroad) to give clients delivery continuity [3].
3. **Legal and tax architecture.** The Diia.City regime and the ongoing shift of specialists from sole-proprietor (FOP) status to employment or gig contracts are changing how work is contracted. Gig contracts through Diia.City rose from 14% to 20% of developers, while the share of individual entrepreneurs among surveyed specialists fell from 62% to 53% between December 2024 and December 2025 [4].
4. **EU convergence.** EU roaming rules took effect in Ukraine on 1 January 2026, and the Ukraine Facility provides financing for 2024–2027, supporting a European orientation [6]. DOU analysis of Q1 2026 likewise describes the European direction as strengthening [9].
5. **New demand pockets.** Startup funding reached about $498 million in 2025, including roughly $302 million for AI-enabled ventures, and around 500 defense-tech and dual-use companies raised about $129 million [6].

## 5. Headwinds

- **Sole-proprietor attrition.** Active IT FOPs fell from a peak of 275,279 in January 2024 to 205,939 in February 2026, down about 21% year over year; new registrations roughly halved to 14,500 [4]. This partly reflects tax and social-contribution changes and partly formalization into other contract forms, so it is not purely a talent loss, but it signals the freelancer layer is thinning [4].
- **Mobilization.** 75% of companies have mobilized employees and 35% employ veterans; firms typically support them with job guarantees or salary compensation [3]. Expect attrition risk in male-heavy teams and a need for backup capacity.
- **Emigration and ageing.** About one in five specialists considers emigrating, and 8% of CEOs plan to close Ukrainian offices against 8% planning to open new ones [3].
- **Infrastructure and security.** Attacks, blackouts and cyber threats continue to affect operations [6].
- **Rising costs vs. flat export revenue,** which squeezes margins for firms selling mainly on price [3].

## 6. Outlook for 2026

Data to date suggest low-single-digit growth:

- **H1 2026:** $3.34 billion (+4.1% y/y); Q2 was $1.70 billion (+3.6% y/y, +3.3% q/q), and June $561 million (+6.7% y/y) [7].
- **Q3 2026:** $1.67 billion (+1.3% y/y); September came in at $552 million (-0.9% y/y) [8].
- Summing these figures gives roughly $5.0 billion for the first nine months (our arithmetic). A full-year 2026 outcome of about $6.8–6.9 billion would require a typically strong Q4; this would be 2–4% above 2025, but still below the 2022 peak. This is our extrapolation, not an official forecast.
- **Market shifts:** H1 growth came from the UAE (+25.3%), Estonia (+19%), Poland (+10.1%), the US (+8.7%) and Cyprus (+8.2%), while Germany (-15.3%), the Netherlands (-14.8%), Israel (-8.3%) and the UK (-5.7%) declined [7]. Through September the US was still growing (+7.6%) and Germany falling (-16.4%) [8].
- **Share of services exports:** computer services were 41.4% of services exports in H1 2026, slightly under 2025's 41.6%, because total services exports grew 5.84% [1][7].
- **Industry sentiment:** the IT Ukraine Association expected stabilization from 2025 and a recovery once at least one decline-causing factor eases [2]. The DOU analysis of Q1 2026 describes a strengthening European direction [9]. BDO frames 2026–2028 as the strategic window for digital recovery but offers no explicit export forecast [6]. Sector forecasts from earlier in 2025 projected turnover of $7.47–7.56 billion, i.e. essentially flat [3].

The central scenario is therefore plateau with mild growth, where upside depends on a ceasefire or security improvements and downside on escalation, further mobilization or tax changes.

## 7. Implications for a Nearshore Partnership Decision

**Reasons to proceed**
- Sector scale ($6.66 billion exports, ~300,000 professionals) gives real vendor choice [1][3].
- Seniority is high (over 88% mid-level or above), supporting complex, high-value engagement models [3].
- Established legal vehicles (Diia.City, with 3,893 residents) and EU alignment ease contracting, IP protection and payment flows [6].
- US and EU clients have kept buying through four years of war, and recent quarters show modest growth [1][7].

**Risks to manage**
- Security and continuity: require business-continuity plans, power and connectivity redundancy, and delivery capacity in Poland, Estonia or elsewhere in the EU, locations that have grown as destinations [3][7].
- Key-person and mobilization risk: contract for team-level, not individual, commitments, with cross-training and bench coverage [3].
- Price pressure: median incomes are rising and the workforce is ageing, so do not assume a deep rate discount [3].
- Structure of the vendor: check whether staff are employees or FOPs/gig contractors, because that shift affects tax, compliance and retention [4].

**Suggested approach.** Start with a 3–6 month pilot with two or three mid-sized service companies (ideally Diia.City residents with an EU entity), define SLAs and continuity clauses, and use data from the pilot to decide on scale-up. Watch the NBU's monthly export releases and Q4 2026 results as the checkpoint for the 2027 plan.
